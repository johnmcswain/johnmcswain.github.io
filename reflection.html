Reflection

If every artifact in this portfolio could only make one claim, it would be this: the adoption of emerging technology in adult learning is directly proportional to the empathy and enthusiasm of those modeling and sharing it. That is not a slogan. It is the pattern I kept running into across nine courses and nine artifacts, and it is the pattern I now design against consciously rather than intuitively.

When I started the M.Ed. program, I thought I already understood educational technology pretty well. I had been designing AI infrastructure for research universities, building training workshops for engineers, and teaching at Georgia Tech. What I didn't fully understand was the theoretical scaffolding underneath those practices: the difference between doing something effectively and being able to articulate why it's effective, which frameworks justify which decisions, and how to design for learning rather than just for capability. The central shift this program produced was to make that scaffolding visible to me. I have language now for things I was already doing, and I have frameworks now for things I was doing badly.

What the program actually changed

I came into the program teaching workshops because I knew the technology. I am leaving it designing instruction because I understand, a little better, how adults change. That is the most honest way I can describe the shift. It shows up across the portfolio in four specific ways that I can now name.

Accessibility moved from something I added at the end to the quality check I start with. The screen reader project in EDET 735 was where that shift became non-negotiable for me. A learning environment that excludes even one learner is not the environment I had been claiming it was, and accessibility is the audit that forces that honesty.

Evaluation moved from asking did they like it? to asking what do they now do differently? The formative evaluation plan from EDET 722 and the monitoring protocol embedded in the EDET 746 procurement plan both operate on that premise. Branch and Varank's (2009, p. 19) insistence that "evaluation initiates the ADDIE process, permeates the ADDIE process, and concludes the ADDIE process" is the sentence I keep coming back to. Evaluation as event was the thing I used to do. Evaluation as ongoing feedback loop is the thing I design for now.

Emerging technology stopped being inherently interesting to me and became something learners must be invited into. That sounds like a small reframing. It is not. It changed what I put on the first slide, how I sequence the first twenty minutes of any workshop, and what I assume about what the learner already cares about. The MGT 7316 course I built at Georgia Tech and the Claude procurement plan I wrote in EDET 746 both reflect that invitation posture.

And the through-line that sits underneath all three: I stopped teaching workshops because I knew the technology and started designing instruction because I understand how adults change. Those are different professions. The program taught me that.

Four commitments I design by

Alongside this program, I have been writing a personal philosophy titled In Spite Of. The title comes from a phrase that recurred across every domain I wrote about (ethics, purpose, identity, suffering, legacy), the commitment to act, to try, to persist, without finishing the sentence, because the specific obstacles do not matter enough to name. That same philosophy maps onto how I design for adult learners, and the mapping is close enough that I want to name it directly here.

Teach in spite of. Adult learners show up tired, skeptical, and overbooked. The job is to design for people who have every reason not to engage, and to do it anyway. This is the premise of every workshop I have ever run at Georgia Tech, Berkeley, Stanford, and NVIDIA, and it is the premise every artifact in this portfolio was built on. It is also the reason the empathy × enthusiasm claim is not optional for me. Without it, the design falls to whichever learner happened to have the most bandwidth that week, and the rest are lost.

Name the cost. Every tool, framework, and method has a real price: cognitive, financial, emotional. Honest instructional design surfaces costs rather than hiding them behind enthusiasm. The Claude procurement plan from EDET 746 was the clearest place I practiced this: licensing, training burden, hardware refresh cycles, and sunset criteria are all in the document on purpose, not because the assignment required them but because a procurement plan that hides its costs is a marketing document. Morrison et al.'s (2019, p. 13) Premise 6, that "the instructional design process focuses on the individual rather than the content," is the reason I now read every proposal I write from the learner's budget backward, not from the technology's capability forward.

More often than not. Mastery in practice is not perfection. It is a genuine, honest majority. Design for the durable average, the Tuesday afternoon, not the demo. This phrase, lifted directly from my personal philosophy, is the standard I now hold my own teaching to. It is also what separates the formative evaluation plan in EDET 722 from a satisfaction survey. A plan built for the honest majority case will catch problems a plan built for the ideal pilot will miss.

Dignity before correction. How knowledge is delivered matters as much as the knowledge itself. Empathy is not a soft skill. It is the transmission medium. This is the commitment that, more than any other, I wish I had understood earlier in my career. Public correction rarely changes minds; it creates defensiveness. Private truth-telling preserves both the information and the relationship. The screen reader project and the accessibility checkpoints in the procurement plan both operationalize this at the institutional level, but the underlying principle is interpersonal. Adult learners can tell the difference between empathy and performance before you finish the first slide.

What I am leaving the program with

I am leaving the University of South Carolina's Learning Design & Technologies program with a working vocabulary for things I was doing before, a set of frameworks that make my prior practice legible to me and to others, and a single sentence I can defend: the success of learning design and emerging technology in instruction is directly proportional to the empathy and enthusiasm of those modeling and sharing it.

Empathy without enthusiasm is condescension. Enthusiasm without empathy is noise. Both fail adult learners. The work, across every artifact in this portfolio, across the procurement plans and lesson plans and evaluations, across the accessible interfaces and the course designs and the workshops I am still running, is to hold both at the same time, more often than not, in spite of whatever the specific day argues against it.

The portfolio is the evidence that I have started doing this deliberately. The rest is the work of a career.
