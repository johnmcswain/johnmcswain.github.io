A manifesto for Lego combat

The most underrated fighting-robot hobby is one motor away from taking off.

Eleven Lego combat robots in three years. One antweight named DVNT that changed how I see all of them. And one unavoidable conclusion about what's holding an entire scene back.

By the founder of Inquisitive Robotics · @InquisitiveRobotics on YouTube · tap a chapter to expand

I've been building Lego combat robots for nearly three years. The names tell the story of the obsession:

Every build, fight, and failure is documented on the channel.

Eleven machines. And every single one was designed around the same constraint — a constraint I didn't fully see until I left the Lego arena for a weekend.

This year I built DVNT and entered the far more popular 1 lb plastic antweight division — and it rearranged how I think about the hobby.

It's not just more popular. It's easier to enter, and the fights are dramatically more intense. There are purpose-built products for every weight class, and with basic CAD skills a robot is — honestly — a thousand times easier to design. You design the bot you imagine, not the bot your parts allow.

Lego combat has everything a great scene needs — accessible materials, endless rebuildability, a passionate community. What it doesn't have is choice in electronics.

From Deviant to Refried Beans, every bot I've built was designed around the same tiny handful of motors — not around the best fighting idea.

That single constraint caps weapon geometry, cramps layouts, and mutes the intensity that makes combat robotics thrilling to watch. The ceiling isn't creativity. It's hardware.

Inquisitive Robotics exists to bring the antweight experience to Lego: smaller, lighter motors, designed for Lego-scale combat, that unlock new electrical layouts and new weapon geometries — the robots the current fleet could never be.

The logic is simple. Better parts make bolder designs possible. Bolder designs make fights more exciting. Excitement grows the audience — and that means more events, more builders, and more fun in a hobby that has always deserved more of all three.

See it, don't take my word for it.
