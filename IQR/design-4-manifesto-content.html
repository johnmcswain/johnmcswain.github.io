Design · Print · Fight

From bricks to CAD: building the fighting robots — and the parts they're missing.

Eleven Lego combat robots. A 3D-printed antweight named DVNT, designed in Fusion 360 and printed in-house. And one conclusion, tested arena after arena: Lego combat is one motor away from taking off.

Planned in Bricklink Studio, driven by BuWizz 3.0 bricks and Mould King motors, rebuilt after every event. Eleven machines — and every single one designed around the same constraint, one I didn't fully see until I left the Lego arena for a weekend.

This year I built DVNT for the far more popular 1 lb plastic antweight division — modeled in Autodesk Fusion 360, printed on a Bambu Lab P2S, and driven by micro brushed ESCs and DC motors picked from a genuinely deep parts catalog.

It rearranged how I think about the hobby. It's not just more popular — it's easier to enter, and the fights are dramatically more intense. Purpose-built products exist for every weight class, and with solid CAD skills a robot is — honestly — a thousand times easier to design. You design the bot you imagine, then choose the parts that serve it: brushed or multirotor ESCs, whatever gearmotor the geometry wants.

You design the bot you imagine. Not the bot your parts allow.

Lego combat has everything a great scene needs — accessible materials, endless rebuildability, a passionate community. What it doesn't have is choice in electronics.

From Deviant to Refried Beans, every bot I've built was designed around the same tiny handful of motors — not around the best fighting idea.

That single constraint caps weapon geometry, cramps layouts, and mutes the intensity that makes combat robotics thrilling to watch. The ceiling isn't creativity. It's hardware.

Inquisitive Robotics exists to bring the antweight experience to Lego: smaller, lighter motors, designed for Lego-scale combat, that unlock new electrical layouts and new weapon geometries — the robots the current fleet could never be.

The same toolchain that builds our antweights — Fusion 360 for design, the P2S for fast iteration — is now pointed at the motor problem itself.

The logic is simple. Better parts make bolder designs possible. Bolder designs make fights more exciting. Excitement grows the audience — and that means more events, more builders, and more fun in a hobby that has always deserved more of all three.
